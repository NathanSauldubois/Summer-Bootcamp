\section*{Easy (7)}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]

\item \textbf{Two uniforms.}
Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent and uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$.
Compute $\mathbb{P}(XY>1/2)$.\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: Heard on the Street, Ch.\ 4 (Statistics Questions), Q4.1 (book p.\ 37}}

\item \textbf{Coin flips: 4 vs 5.}
You flip 4 fair coins; your opponent flips 5 fair coins. You win if you get strictly more heads than your opponent.
What is $\mathbb{P}(\text{you win})$?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: Heard on the Street, Ch.\ 4, Q4.3 (book p.\ 37}}

\item \textbf{Two kings.}
Two cards are dealt uniformly at random from a standard 52-card deck (without replacement). There are exactly 4 kings.
What is the probability both cards are kings?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: Heard on the Street, Ch.\ 4, Q4.12 (book p.\ 39}}

\item \textbf{Coin toss game (symmetry).}
Gambler $A$ flips $n+1$ fair coins and gambler $B$ flips $n$ fair coins.
What is the probability that $A$ gets more heads than $B$?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews, Ch.\ 4, ``Coin toss game'' (book p.\ 61)}}

\item \textbf{Card game.}
A casino shuffles a standard 52-card deck. You draw one card, then the dealer draws one card without replacement.
If your card’s rank is higher, you win; otherwise (tie or lower), the house wins.
What is your probability of winning?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews, Ch.\ 4, ``Card game'' (book p.\ 61)}}

\item \textbf{Three dice in increasing order.}
Roll a fair die three times. What is the probability that the results are in \emph{strictly} increasing order?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews, Ch.\ 4, ``Dice order'' (book p.\ 78)}}

\item \textbf{Standard deviation warm-up.}
Compute the standard deviation of the dataset $(1,2,3,4,5)$ (population standard deviation).\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: Heard on the Street, Ch.\ 4, Q4.20 (book p.\ 41)}}

\end{enumerate}

\section*{Medium (7)}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}, resume]

\item \textbf{Two jars, 50 white \& 50 black.}
You have two empty jars and 100 marbles: 50 white, 50 black.
You may distribute the marbles between the two jars however you like.
Then (after mixing and the jars being randomly permuted so you can’t tell which is which), you choose one jar and draw one marble.
How should you distribute the marbles to maximize the probability of drawing a white marble?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: Heard on the Street, Ch.\ 4, Q4.15 (book p.\ 40)}}

\item \textbf{Two-headed coin posterior (Bayes).}
A jar contains 999 fair coins and 1 two-headed coin.
You pick one coin uniformly at random and flip it 10 times; all flips are heads.
What is the probability you picked the two-headed coin?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: Heard on the Street, Ch.\ 4, Q4.22 (book p.\ 41)}}

\item \textbf{Medical test (base rates).}
A disease occurs with probability $0.5\%$ in the population.
A test is \emph{perfectly sensitive}: if you have the disease, it is positive with probability 1.
It has a $7\%$ false-positive rate: if you do not have the disease, it is positive with probability $0.07$.
A random person tests positive. What is the probability they have the disease?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: Heard on the Street, Ch.\ 4, Q4.30 (book p.\ 43)}}

\item \textbf{Drunk passenger.}
100 passengers have assigned seats $1,\dots,100$.
Passenger 1 is drunk and chooses a random seat uniformly.
Each later passenger takes their own seat if available; otherwise they choose uniformly among remaining free seats.
You are passenger 100. What is the probability you end up in seat 100?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews, Ch.\ 4, ``Drunk passenger'' (book p.\ 62)}}

\item \textbf{Five letters, five envelopes (mis-mailing).}
You address 5 letters to 5 firms and your child puts the letters into envelopes uniformly at random (a random permutation).
What is the probability that \emph{all 5} letters end up in the wrong envelopes?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews, Ch.\ 4, ``Application letters'' (book p.\ 69--70)}}

\item \textbf{Two children, you meet a boy.}
A colleague has two children. You see them walking with one child, and that child is a boy.
Assume independent genders with probability $1/2$ each. What is the probability both children are boys?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews, Ch.\ 4, ``Boys and girls'' Part B (book p.\ 73)}}

\item \textbf{Monty Hall.}
Three doors: behind one is a prize, behind two are goats.
You pick one door. The host, who knows where the prize is, opens one of the other two doors to reveal a goat, then offers you the option to switch to the remaining unopened door.
Should you switch, and what is your win probability if you do?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews, Ch.\ 4, ``Monty Hall problem'' (book p.\ 78)}}

\end{enumerate}

\section*{Hard (6)}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}, resume]

\item \textbf{Russian roulette with two adjacent bullets.}
A 6-chamber revolver has 2 \emph{adjacent} loaded chambers and 4 empty chambers.
The cylinder is spun once, the trigger is pulled and you hear a click (you survived).
Before pulling the trigger again, do you prefer to (i) spin the cylinder again, or (ii) not spin and just pull?
Which choice maximizes survival probability for the next shot?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: Heard on the Street, Ch.\ 4, Q4.21 (book p.\ 41)}}

\item \textbf{Chocolate chips into 100 cookies (occupancy).}
You add $N$ chocolate chips uniformly at random into cookie dough and then split the dough into 100 equal cookies.
Assume each chip independently ends up in a uniformly random cookie.
How large should $N$ be to make the probability at least $90\%$ that \emph{every} cookie has at least one chip?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: Heard on the Street, Ch.\ 4, Q4.32 (book p.\ 43)}}

\item \textbf{Expected time to reach Box 4 (Markov chain).}
There are four boxes in a row labeled 1--4. You start in Box 1.
Flip a fair coin:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item From Box 1: Heads $\to$ Box 2, Tails $\to$ Box 3.
\item From Box 2: Heads $\to$ Box 1, Tails $\to$ Box 4 (and the game ends).
\item From Box 3: follow the same rule as from Box 1 (Heads $\to$ Box 2, Tails $\to$ Box 3), and when you reach Box 4 the game ends.
\end{itemize}
What is the expected number of coin tosses needed to reach Box 4?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: Heard on the Street, Ch.\ 4, Q4.34 (book p.\ 43)}}

\item \textbf{$N$ random points on a circle.}
Choose $N$ points independently and uniformly at random on the circumference of a circle.
What is the probability that all $N$ points lie within some (closed) semicircle?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews, Ch.\ 4, ``$N$ points on a circle'' (book p.\ 63)}}

\item \textbf{Single-elimination tournament (top two meeting).}
A knockout tournament has $2^n$ players with strict ranking (player 1 best, then 2, etc.).
Each round pairs remaining players uniformly at random. Assume better player always wins any match.
What is the probability that players 1 and 2 meet in the final?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews, Ch.\ 4, ``Chess tournament'' (book p.\ 68)}}

\item \textbf{Pirates, locks, and keys (combinatorial design).}
A pirate group has 11 pirates and wants to protect treasure in a safe with multiple locks.
Any 6 pirates together must be able to open the safe, but no group of 5 pirates should be able to open it.
Keys can be copied and given to multiple pirates; each lock may have multiple keys.
What is the minimum number of locks needed, and how many keys must each pirate carry in this optimal scheme?\\
\hfill{\small\textit{Source: A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews, Ch.\ 4, ``Screwy pirates'' (book p.\ 67)}}

\end{enumerate}
